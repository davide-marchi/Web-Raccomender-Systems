\section{Week 6 [Feb 3 - Feb 9]: Familiarize Yourself with the Datasets}

This week focused on understanding the dataset and preparing it for recommendation tasks. The goal was to clean the data, explore its properties, and extract key statistics to gain insights into user-item interactions. This preprocessing step was essential to ensure data quality for later modeling.

\subsection{Dataset Description and Import}
The dataset used in this project is the ``Musical Instruments'' dataset from the Amazon Review data 2023 (5-core subset). The dataset consists of 14,000 item ratings from nearly 900 users on approximately 500 items. The data is split into training and test sets, with 80\% of the data allocated for training (``train.tsv'') and 20\% for testing (``test.tsv'').

The dataset was imported using the Python library \texttt{pandas}\cite{pandas} and its \texttt{read\_csv} function.

\subsection{Data Cleaning and Preprocessing}
To ensure the quality of the dataset, I performed the following preprocessing steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Removal of missing values (entries with missing user ID, item ID, or rating).
    \item Deduplication by keeping the most recent rating when a user rated the same item multiple times.
    \item Ensuring that all users in the test set are also present in the training set.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion}
The dataset exhibits common characteristics of user-item interactions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item There is a long-tail distribution where a small number of items receive most of the ratings, while many items have very few ratings.
    \item The majority of users have rated only a very small percentage of the available items, which can lead to sparsity issues in collaborative filtering approaches.
    \item Few items dominate the high-rated category, which may bias recommendation systems and favor a TopPop approach.
    \item Most ratings are 5/5, likely because users tend to rate items they like more than those they dislike. This results in a highly imbalanced dataset for the task of predicting ratings.
\end{itemize}

\section{Week 7 [Feb 10 - Feb 16]: Collaborative Filtering Recommender System}

This week focused on implementing collaborative filtering recommender systems. The goal was to build models that predict user preferences based on past ratings, compare different approaches, and optimize their performance through hyperparameter tuning. These models serve as the foundation for generating personalized recommendations.

\subsection{Top Popular (TopPop) Recommender System}

As a baseline, I first implemented the \textbf{Top Popular (TopPop) recommender system}. This model recommends the most popular items based on the number of ``high'' ratings (ratings $\geq$ 3) in the training set. I computed the frequency of high ratings per item and selected the top-k items as recommendations for all users.

While computationally inexpensive, this method lacks personalization since it does not consider individual user preferences.

\subsection{Collaborative Filtering Models}

I then used to Scikit-Surprise library\cite{surprise} to be able to compare it with one neighborhood-based model and one latent factor model. The ones I chose are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User-based k-Nearest Neighbors with Means (KNNWithMeans)}: A neighborhood-based approach that identifies similar users based on their rating history.
    \item \textbf{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}: A latent factor model that decomposes the user-item interaction matrix into lower-dimensional representations.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning and Evaluation}

To optimize both models and finde the best hyperparameters, I performed a Grid Search with \textbf{5-fold cross-validation} on the training set. The parameters I tuned included:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{KNNWithMeans}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{k}: [5, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 70, 100]
        \item \textbf{sim\_options}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{name}: ['cosine', 'msd']
            \item \textbf{user\_based}: [True]
        \end{itemize}
            
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SVD}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{n\_factors}: [3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100]
        \item \textbf{n\_epochs}: [5, 10, 20, 50, 100]
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The evaluation metric chosen was \textbf{Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)}. RMSE penalizes larger errors more than smaller ones, making it a good measure to
capture the overall predictive accuracy of our recommendation models.
Lower RMSE indicates that the model predictions are closer to the actual ratings.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

The best hyperparameters for each model can be found in \autoref{tab:best_hyperparameters}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\resizebox{1\columnwidth}{!}{
\begin{tabular}{lll}

\hline
KNNWithMeans & {'k': 70, 'sim\_options': \{'name': 'cosine', 'user\_based': True\}} & 0.9100 \\

The SVD model, despite it's low dimensional latent space, outperformed the KNNWithMeans model with a lower RMSE of 0.8440 compared to 0.9100. This indicates that the latent factor model is better at capturing the underlying patterns in the user-item interaction matrix.

After that, both models were trained again on the whole training set to ensure that no data available for training was left behind before the evaluation on the test set.

\section{Week 8 [Feb 17 - Feb 23]: Evaluation of Recommender Systems}

This week focused on evaluating the performance of the collaborative filtering models implemented in Week 7. The evaluation aimed to measure both the accuracy of rating predictions and the effectiveness of recommendations.

\subsection{RMSE Evaluation}

The first step in evaluating the models was measuring the \textbf{Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)} on the test set. RMSE quantifies how close the predicted ratings are to the actual ratings, with lower values indicating better accuracy. The trained models were tested on the preprocessed test data, and their RMSE values were computed and are shown in \autoref{tab:RMSE_comparison}.

While RMSE is useful for evaluating rating prediction accuracy, it has some limitations. It does not consider ranking quality and does not directly reflect recommendation usefulness. For this reason, additional ranking-based metrics were computed.

\subsection{Ranking-based Evaluation Metrics}

To assess the quality of the generated recommendations, the models were evaluated using the following metrics:


\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hit Rate}: Measures how often at least one relevant item appears in the top-k recommendations.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Advantages:} Simple, easy to interpret.
        \item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Doesn't consider ranking or multiple relevant items.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Precision@k}: Proportion of recommended items in the top-k list that are relevant.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Advantages:} Direct measure of recommendation quality.
        \item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Ignores total relevant items available.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{MAP@k}: Averages precision at positions of relevant items, rewarding good ranking.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Advantages:} Considers ranking order.
        \item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Sensitive to users with few relevant items.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{MRR@k}: Focuses on the rank of the first relevant item. Computed as one divided by its rank.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Advantages:} Rewards early relevant recommendations.
        \item \textbf{Disadvantages:} Ignores additional relevant items.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Coverage}: Proportion of unique items recommended at least once.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Advantages:} Indicates diversity in recommendations.
        \item \textbf{Disadvantages:} High coverage doesn’t mean better relevance.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The results of the computation of these metrics are shown in \autoref{tab:evaluation_metrics}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \resizebox{1\columnwidth}{!}{
        \begin{tabular}{llllll}

            \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Hit Rate} & \textbf{Precision@10} & \textbf{MAP@10} & \textbf{MRR@10} & \textbf{Coverage} \\
            \hline
            KNNWithMeans & 0.1092 & 0.0117 & 0.0084 & 0.0314 & 0.5874 \\

    \label{tab:evaluation_metrics}
\end{table}

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hit Rate:} Surprisingly, the \textbf{TopPop model} achieved the highest hit rate, likely because popular items frequently appear in the test set.
    \item \textbf{Precision@10:} The TopPop model outperformed the collaborative filtering methods in precision as well, suggesting that highly rated items tend to be good general recommendations.
    \item \textbf{MAP@10 and MRR@10:} TopPop also performed best in these ranking-based metrics, showing that the most popular items are often relevant.
    \item \textbf{Coverage:} The \textbf{kNN model} had the highest coverage (58.7\%), indicating that it provided more diverse recommendations, while SVD had lower coverage. The TopPop model had the lowest coverage, meaning it recommended only a small subset of items.
\end{itemize}

The evaluation results show an interesting (but expected) trade-off:
The \textbf{TopPop model} is simple but performs surprisingly well, especially in terms of hit rate and ranking metrics. However, it lacks personalization.
The \textbf{kNN model} achieves the highest coverage, meaning it suggests a wider variety of items, which could be useful for expanding user interest.
The \textbf{SVD model} despite achieving the lowest RMSE, performed worse than expected in ranking-based evaluation, possibly due to the sparsity of the dataset.

These results suggest that, while collaborative filtering methods are typically expected to perform well, the characteristics of the dataset (such as sparsity and rating distribution) play a significant role in determining effectiveness.

In the end we do not have a clear winner, as the best model depends on the specific use case and the trade-offs between accuracy, diversity, and coverage.
